Becoming Orthodox

For catechumens & inquirers

Coming into the Orthodox Church is a journey.

Along the way you will probably have many questions — some theological, some practical, and some as simple as “What am I supposed to do next?”

I have a question for Fr. John

Questions are welcome.

This page gathers the questions that come up again and again for inquirers and catechumens at the Nativity, and says how we go about the journey into the Church here.

Where a question has been answered, the answer is on the page. Where it has not, the question is here anyway, with a note saying that it is waiting on Fr. John — because a question you were about to ask is worth seeing even before anyone has written the answer down.

A note from the beginning

This page is a guide, not a substitute for your relationship with your priest.

Every person's circumstances are different. When Fr. John gives you guidance for your own situation, follow his guidance rather than a general answer on this page.

Getting started

Before anything is decided

Nothing here commits you to anything. People ask questions at this parish for a long time before any decision is made, and some go on asking happily for years.

What is an inquirer?

Someone who is asking questions about the Orthodox Church. Nothing is being asked of you in return.

An inquirer is simply someone who is looking into the Orthodox faith — coming to services, reading, asking questions, and seeing what the life of the Church is like from the inside.

No commitment is implied. Inquirers' conversations happen quietly and at your own pace, often over many months. You are welcome to keep coming, keep reading, and take as long as you need.

What is a catechumen?

Someone the Church has formally set on the path towards being received, and who is being taught along the way.

A catechumen is someone who has moved from looking on to preparing: the Church has received them as a learner, prays for them by name in her services, and teaches them in the time before their baptism or chrismation. The word is as old as the Church herself.

How a person becomes a catechumen here, and what that time looks like at the Nativity, is Fr. John's to describe rather than ours.

Your patron saint

Choosing your patron saint

A saint whose life you come to know, whose feast becomes your own day, and whose prayers you ask for as long as you live. Read, pray, and talk it over with Fr. John and your godparent.

A Cloud of Witnesses is a free resource made by members of this parish: a searchable communion of the saints and the calendar of the Church. Its patron saint quiz is a gentle way to meet saints you have not heard of — a place to start, not a decision.

Why do Orthodox Christians have patron saints?

Because the saints are alive in Christ, and we ask for their prayers as we would ask a friend's.

Orthodox Christians honour the saints and ask for their prayers. A patron saint is one of them given to you by name: someone whose life you come to know, whose feast becomes your own day, and whose prayers you ask for as long as you live.

A Cloud of Witnesses, a free resource made by members of this parish, is a good place to begin reading about them.

What is a name day?

The feast of your patron saint — the day the Church keeps their memory, and with it, yours.

Your name day is the feast of the saint whose name you bear. Among Orthodox Christians it is greeted much as a birthday is, and often more warmly: “Many years!” is the thing to say.

The Church's calendar of the saints, day by day, is on A Cloud of Witnesses.

Can I receive Holy Communion?

Not yet. Holy Communion is received by Orthodox Christians who are properly prepared — which is what you are preparing for.

Holy Communion is received by Orthodox Christians who are properly prepared to receive the Eucharist. Until you are received into the Church, you do not come to the chalice — and that is not a judgement on you, it is the thing the whole catechumenate is pointed towards.

When your first Communion will be is part of your reception, and Fr. John will tell you.

Can I receive antidoron?

Yes. Antidoron is blessed bread that anyone present may receive, including those who are not Orthodox.

Antidoron is blessed bread that anyone may receive, including visitors who are not Orthodox. It is normally given by the priest after the Divine Liturgy concludes, as people leave for the hall — so you will pass him on your way out, and you are welcome to take a piece.

It is not Holy Communion. It is bread that has been blessed, given as a sign of the shared life of the parish.

Can I light candles?

Yes. A candle is a small offering made with a prayer, and you are welcome to light one.

A candle is a small offering made with a prayer — for someone you love, for someone who has died, or for yourself. The flame is also an image: Christ is the Light of the world, and a lit candle is one of the Church's oldest ways of saying so.

You are welcome to light one when you come. You are equally welcome not to.

Can I venerate icons?

Yes. Venerating an icon is something like kissing a photograph of someone you love.

Orthodox Christians venerate icons; we do not worship them. Worship belongs to God alone, and the honour given to an icon passes to the person shown in it, not to the wood and paint.

Nobody is watching to see whether you do it, or checking that you do it correctly. Follow the person in front of you, or simply stand and pray.

Can I make the sign of the Cross?

Yes — it is prayer made with the body, not a gesture that has to be got right.

The thumb and first two fingers are joined to confess the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit — three, and one. The remaining two fingers are folded to the palm, confessing that Christ is both God and man.

It is prayer made with the body, not a gesture that has to be got right, and nobody is expected to join in before they are ready.

When are services?

Every Sunday: Orthros at 9:00 a.m., Divine Liturgy at 10:00 a.m. Weekday and feast-day services are on the calendar.

Sunday morning is Orthros at 9:00 a.m., followed by the Divine Liturgy at 10:00 a.m. They are two distinct services; people arrive across both.

Answer last reviewed 13 August 2026

What is Orthros?

The morning service that comes before the Divine Liturgy. Here it begins at 9:00 a.m.

Orthros is the Church's morning prayer — psalms, hymns for the day, and the Gospel of the Resurrection on a Sunday. It leads straight into the Divine Liturgy without a break, which is why the two can sound like one long service.

You are not late if you arrive during it, and you are not late if you arrive after it either.

What is the Divine Liturgy?

The Sunday service of the Church, at whose heart is Holy Communion. Here it begins at 10:00 a.m.

The Divine Liturgy is the central service of the Orthodox Church: scripture, prayer and hymns leading to the offering of the Eucharist. Most Sundays here it is the Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom.

Services are approximately 50% Greek and 50% English, and congregational portions — the Creed, the Lord's Prayer — are offered in more than one language.

Anywhere. There is no reserved seating.

Take a Greek-English Divine Liturgy book from a greeter or from the pew. It stays in the church.

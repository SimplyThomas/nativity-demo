Visiting

Your first Sunday, explained

Visiting an Orthodox church for the first time can feel unfamiliar. You don't need to know the prayers, when to stand, or what to do before you arrive. One of our greeters will be in the narthex to welcome you, and a Greek-English Divine Liturgy book will be available so you can follow along if you would like.

Step one

Before you come

Four questions people ask before a first visit. None of the answers is homework — open the ones that would set your mind at rest and ignore the rest.

What should I wear?

“Dress like you are meeting someone important.” — Fr. John

Orthodox Christians approach worship with reverence, and our clothing can reflect that. Choose something modest and respectful — but don't let anxiety about what you are wearing keep you from coming.

You do not need special clothing to visit, and you do not need to buy anything beforehand. In our parish you will see people dressed in a range of ways. The point is reverence, not a uniform.

What about head coverings?

The practice of Christian women covering their heads during prayer has ancient roots and is mentioned by St. Paul in 1 Corinthians 11. It continues among many Orthodox Christian women today, although customs differ between parishes and cultures.

At our parish, many women veil and many do not. Either is perfectly comfortable here. If you normally veil for prayer, please feel comfortable doing so. If you do not, please feel equally comfortable attending without a head covering.

Visitors should never feel that they need to buy or wear a veil before attending.

Most importantly: please come. We would much rather have you worship with us than stay home because you were worried about what to wear.

What should I bring?

Just yourself.

You don't need to bring anything to attend an Orthodox service. Just come.

If you are used to a Protestant church, you may be accustomed to carrying a Bible on a Sunday. You are welcome to bring one, and you do not need it to follow the Divine Liturgy: Scripture is woven all through Orthodox worship, and the appointed Epistle and Gospel for the day are proclaimed aloud during the service.

If you would like, you can read the day's Scripture readings before you arrive.

Today's Scripture readings →

A Greek-English Divine Liturgy book will be available to help you follow the service. One of our greeters will be happy to help you find one. The books belong to the church and stay here at the end of the service, so please leave yours in the pew as you go.

No Bible to pack. No supplies to bring. No homework to finish. Just come and worship with us.

What language is the service in?

About half Greek, about half English. You do not need to know Greek.

As a Greek Orthodox parish, our services are roughly half in Greek and half in English. The Nicene Creed, the Lord's Prayer and other parts the congregation prays together are offered in more than one language, so that everyone gathered can pray them.

A Greek-English Divine Liturgy book will be available, with the service set out in both languages, so you can follow along if you would like.

You don't have to keep up

Orthodox worship can be a lot to take in at first. You may find yourself hunting for every hymn and response in the book while the service has already moved on. If that happens, stop searching. Close the book for a while. Look around, listen, pray, and let the Liturgy carry you.

Follow along when it's helpful. Listen and observe when it isn't. Participate as you become comfortable.

Words you may begin to recognise

Κύριε, ἐλέησον

Kyrie eleison — “Lord, have mercy.”

You'll hear this response again and again throughout Orthodox worship. Even if you know no other Greek, it probably won't be long before you recognise Kyrie eleison.

The congregation also recites the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed — often simply called the Creed — an ancient statement of the Christian faith professed at every Divine Liturgy. Both it and the Lord's Prayer are set out below if you would like to read them beforehand.

Read the Nicene Creed

The Creed was set down by the Councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) to say plainly what the Church believes about God the Father, Jesus Christ, and the Holy Spirit. Orthodox Christians confess it together at every Divine Liturgy — the same words the Church has prayed for more than sixteen centuries.

I believe in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth, and of all things visible and invisible.

And in one Lord Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, begotten of the Father before all ages; Light of Light, true God of true God, begotten, not created, of one essence with the Father, through Whom all things were made.

Who for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven and was incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary and became man.

He was crucified for us under Pontius Pilate, and suffered and was buried; and He rose on the third day, according to the Scriptures.

He ascended into heaven and is seated at the right hand of the Father; and He will come again with glory to judge the living and the dead. His kingdom shall have no end.

And in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the Giver of Life, Who proceeds from the Father, Who together with the Father and the Son is worshipped and glorified, Who spoke through the prophets.

In one, holy, catholic, and apostolic Church. I confess one baptism for the forgiveness of sins. I look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life of the age to come. Amen.

Read the Lord's Prayer

The prayer Christ taught His disciples. The congregation prays it aloud together near the end of the Divine Liturgy, and the priest concludes with the doxology: “For Thine is the kingdom and the power and the glory, of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit…”

Our Father, Who art in heaven, hallowed be Thy name. Thy kingdom come, Thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread; and forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us; and lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one.

Πάτερ ἡμῶν ὁ ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς, ἁγιασθήτω τὸ ὄνομά σου· ἐλθέτω ἡ βασιλεία σου· γενηθήτω τὸ θέλημά σου, ὡς ἐν οὐρανῷ καὶ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς. Τὸν ἄρτον ἡμῶν τὸν ἐπιούσιον δὸς ἡμῖν σήμερον· καὶ ἄφες ἡμῖν τὰ ὀφειλήματα ἡμῶν, ὡς καὶ ἡμεῖς ἀφίεμεν τοῖς ὀφειλέταις ἡμῶν· καὶ μὴ εἰσενέγκῃς ἡμᾶς εἰς πειρασμόν, ἀλλὰ ῥῦσαι ἡμᾶς ἀπὸ τοῦ πονηροῦ.

You don't need to understand every word to come and pray with us. Take your time. Listen. Observe. Follow along when you can. There is no test, and no one expects you to know what to do on your first Sunday.

Step two

When you arrive

Enter in peace. We'll help with the rest.

The Church has long been described as a hospital for the soul. None of us comes because we have everything figured out.

When you first come in, you will find yourself in the narthex — the entrance area just before the main worship space, which is called the nave. You will see icons and candles, and, most Sundays, a greeter waiting to welcome you and point you the right way.

You are welcome to light a candle, venerate the icons, or simply walk in and find a seat. If those practices are unfamiliar, there is no expectation that you join in. Take your time and do what feels comfortable.

Occasionally you may arrive during a portion of the service when we ask those entering to wait quietly in the narthex. Don't worry about knowing when to enter — one of our greeters will be happy to guide you.

There is no reserved seating. Sit wherever you like, and ask the person next to you if you are unsure about something — people are glad to be asked. Orthodox services include standing, sitting, bowing and making the sign of the Cross, and you should not feel you have to keep pace with everyone around you. Sit when you need to. Stand when you're comfortable. Watch, listen and pray.

Most of all, enter in peace.

We come seeking healing, mercy and life in Christ. You are welcome to come and seek Him with us.

Step three

Sunday morning, step by step

What actually happens, in the order it happens. Sunday runs from Orthros through to coffee hour, and you are welcome at every part of it.

01
9:00 AM

Orthros

The morning prayer service

Orthros, also called Matins, is the Church's morning service of prayer and preparation before the Divine Liturgy. It includes psalms, hymns, Scripture and prayers reflecting the feast or the saint commemorated that day.

Visitors are welcome at Orthros, but should not feel obliged to begin a first visit here. Many inquirers, and many families with young children, arrive closer to the beginning of the Divine Liturgy.

02
10:00 AM

Divine Liturgy

The principal worship service of Sunday

The Divine Liturgy is the central worship service of the Orthodox Church, where the parish gathers in prayer and celebrates the Holy Eucharist. Expect ancient prayers and hymns, readings from Scripture, incense, icons, and stretches when the congregation stands.

You are welcome to take part in the prayers and the worship. Don't worry about knowing when to stand, sit, make the sign of the Cross or venerate an icon. You are equally welcome simply to observe and pray.

03
Within the Liturgy

Holy Communion

A note for our visitors

Holy Communion is received by Orthodox Christians who are properly prepared to receive the Eucharist. If you are not Orthodox, please remain in your place while Communion is given.

This is not a judgement about you, and it is not a way of keeping you at arm's length. Orthodox Christians understand the Eucharist as the expression of a unity of faith already shared — so receiving it together says something the Church cannot yet say. You remain entirely welcome to worship and to pray with us, and there is blessed bread for you a few minutes later.

If you are unsure what to do at any point, ask the person who greeted you. Nobody minds the question.

04
As the Liturgy ends

Antidoron

Blessed bread — and anyone may receive it

Antidoron is blessed bread that everyone may receive, including visitors who are not Orthodox. Fr. John normally hands it out as the Divine Liturgy concludes and people leave the church on their way to the hall — so you will pass him, and you are welcome to take a piece.

Antidoron is not Holy Communion. It is bread that has been blessed, given as a sign of the shared life of the parish, and offered to everyone present.

05
During the school year

Sunday School

They aren't leaving for the day

You may notice many of our children leaving together after Holy Communion has been served. They are going to their Sunday School classes, which continue until coffee hour, when the children rejoin their families in the hall.

Visiting with children? See bringing children below, and please ask a greeter when you arrive.

06
After Liturgy

Coffee hour

Please stay and eat with us.

Our Sunday doesn't end when the service does. After the Divine Liturgy we gather in the parish hall for coffee hour — a shared, potluck-style meal, and an old Christian habit of sitting down to eat together after worship.

Visitors are warmly invited, and you do not need to bring anything. This is the easiest place to meet parishioners, ask the ordinary questions you didn't want to ask during the service, and find out what this parish is actually like.

07
Before you leave

The parish bookstore

The bookstore is open every Sunday after the Divine Liturgy, and you are welcome to browse. It carries Orthodox books and pamphlets, prayer books, icons, prayer ropes, incense, crosses, candles and other devotional items for the home — including books written for people just beginning to learn about the Orthodox faith.

What about bringing children?

Bring them. Children belong in the life of the Church.

Children of every age are welcome in our services. Orthodox worship is something we experience together as a family, and children learn to worship by being present for worship — seeing the icons, hearing the hymns, smelling the incense, making the sign of the Cross, growing into the rhythm of the Church alongside the rest of us.

Christ welcomed little children and taught that we too must become like them. Their presence among us is not an interruption to worship; they are part of our parish family and part of our worship.

Babies cry. Toddlers wriggle. Children whisper, drop things and need to move about. Please don't worry if yours make noise — no one expects them to be perfectly quiet for an entire service.

If your child needs a break, you are always welcome to step out and come back when you're ready. But please never feel that you have to leave simply because a child is being a child.

If a full Sunday morning would be difficult for your family, many families with young children arrive closer to the beginning of the Divine Liturgy rather than staying for all of Orthros beforehand.

Sunday School

During the school year, parish children attending Sunday School leave for class after Holy Communion has been served, and stay in class until coffee hour begins. Whether a visiting child may join a class that same day has not been settled — if you are visiting with children, please ask a greeter when you arrive and they will help you.

Your children are welcome here, too.

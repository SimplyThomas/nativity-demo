Visiting

Your first Sunday, explained

Visiting an Orthodox church for the first time can feel unfamiliar. You don't need to know the prayers, when to stand, or what to do before you arrive. Someone will be there to welcome you and help you find your way, and there is a Divine Liturgy book in the pew so you can follow along if you would like.

Sunday Morning, Step by Step
A practical note

When should I arrive?

If you would like to experience the full Sunday morning cycle of worship, join us for Orthros at 9:00 AM. If this is your first visit—especially if you are bringing young children—you may want to come closer to the beginning of Divine Liturgy, which typically begins at 10:00 AM.

9:00 AM

Orthros

The morning prayer service

Orthros, also called Matins, is the Church's morning service of prayer and preparation before the Divine Liturgy. It includes psalms, hymns, Scripture, and prayers that reflect the feast or saint being commemorated that day.

Visitors are welcome to attend Orthros, but they should not feel obligated to begin their first visit here. Many inquirers and families with young children choose to arrive closer to the beginning of the Divine Liturgy.

02
10:00 AM

Divine Liturgy

The principal worship service of Sunday

The Divine Liturgy is the central worship service of the Orthodox Church, when the parish gathers in prayer and celebrates the Holy Eucharist. Expect ancient prayers and hymns, readings from Scripture, incense, icons, and periods when the congregation stands.

Visitors are welcome to participate in the prayers and worship. Don't worry about knowing when to stand, sit, make the sign of the Cross, or venerate an icon. You are welcome to simply observe and pray.

03
Within the Liturgy

Holy Communion

A note for our visitors

The Eucharist is received by baptized and chrismated Orthodox Christians who are properly prepared to receive Holy Communion. If you are not Orthodox, please remain in place during Communion. This does not mean that you are unwelcome; Orthodox Christians understand Communion as an expression of full sacramental unity in the faith.

Visitors are welcome to receive antidoron, the blessed bread shared with the faithful and visitors. If you are unsure what to do at any point, simply ask the person who greeted you.

04
After Liturgy

The Agape Meal

Please stay and eat with us.

Our Sunday gathering doesn't end when the service does. After Divine Liturgy, we gather for our Agape Meal—a shared, potluck-style meal in the parish hall.

Visitors are warmly invited to join us. You do not need to bring anything. This is a wonderful opportunity to meet parishioners, ask questions, and get to know our community.

Before you leave

While you are here, you are welcome to browse our parish bookstore. It includes books for those learning about the Orthodox faith, spiritual reading, pamphlets, prayer books, and other Orthodox devotional and worship items.

What Language Is the Service In?

Our services are in both English and Greek.

As a Greek Orthodox parish, you'll hear both English and Greek throughout the Divine Liturgy. Don't worry—you do not need to know Greek to worship with us.

You'll find a Divine Liturgy book in the pews, with the service in both Greek and English, so you can follow along if you'd like.

You don't have to keep up

Orthodox worship can feel like a lot to take in at first. You may find yourself trying to locate every hymn, response, and prayer in the book while the service has already moved ahead. If that happens, don't worry about finding your place. Close the book for a while. Look around, listen, pray, and experience the Liturgy.

Follow along when it's helpful. Observe when it isn't. Participate as you become comfortable.

Words you may begin to recognize

Κύριε, ἐλέησον

Kyrie eleison — “Lord, have mercy.”

You'll hear this response frequently throughout Orthodox worship. Even if you don't know any other Greek, it probably won't be long before you recognize Kyrie eleison.

You'll also hear the congregation pray some familiar prayers together. The Lord's Prayer (“Our Father”) may be prayed in multiple languages, reflecting the people gathered in our parish.

The congregation also recites the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed, often simply called the Nicene Creed or the Creed. It is an ancient statement of the Christian faith professed in Orthodox worship.

Read the Nicene Creed

The Creed was set down by the Councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) to say plainly what the Church believes about God the Father, Jesus Christ, and the Holy Spirit. Orthodox Christians confess it together at every Divine Liturgy — the same words the Church has prayed for more than sixteen centuries.

I believe in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth, and of all things visible and invisible.

And in one Lord Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, begotten of the Father before all ages; Light of Light, true God of true God, begotten, not created, of one essence with the Father, through Whom all things were made.

Who for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven and was incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary and became man.

He was crucified for us under Pontius Pilate, and suffered and was buried; and He rose on the third day, according to the Scriptures.

He ascended into heaven and is seated at the right hand of the Father; and He will come again with glory to judge the living and the dead. His kingdom shall have no end.

And in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the Giver of Life, Who proceeds from the Father, Who together with the Father and the Son is worshipped and glorified, Who spoke through the prophets.

In one, holy, catholic, and apostolic Church. I confess one baptism for the forgiveness of sins. I look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life of the age to come. Amen.

Read the Lord's Prayer

The prayer Christ taught His disciples. The congregation prays it aloud together near the end of the Divine Liturgy, and the priest concludes with the doxology: “For Thine is the kingdom and the power and the glory, of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit…”

Our Father, Who art in heaven, hallowed be Thy name. Thy kingdom come, Thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread; and forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us; and lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one.

Πάτερ ἡμῶν ὁ ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς, ἁγιασθήτω τὸ ὄνομά σου· ἐλθέτω ἡ βασιλεία σου· γενηθήτω τὸ θέλημά σου, ὡς ἐν οὐρανῷ καὶ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς. Τὸν ἄρτον ἡμῶν τὸν ἐπιούσιον δὸς ἡμῖν σήμερον· καὶ ἄφες ἡμῖν τὰ ὀφειλήματα ἡμῶν, ὡς καὶ ἡμεῖς ἀφίεμεν τοῖς ὀφειλέταις ἡμῶν· καὶ μὴ εἰσενέγκῃς ἡμᾶς εἰς πειρασμόν, ἀλλὰ ῥῦσαι ἡμᾶς ἀπὸ τοῦ πονηροῦ.

You don't need to understand every word to come and pray with us. Take your time. Listen. Observe. Follow along when you can. There is no test, and no one expects you to know what to do on your first Sunday.

What Should I Wear?

Come dressed with modesty and reverence — but don't let your clothing keep you from coming.

Orthodox Christians traditionally dress modestly for church, recognizing that we are entering a sacred place and gathering for worship. You do not need special clothing to visit, and you do not need to purchase anything before attending.

In our parish, you will see people dressed in different ways. Some wear dresses, skirts, or more formal clothing, while others wear modest slacks or other everyday attire. Men likewise range from suits and dress shirts to more casual but respectful clothing. The important thing is to choose clothing that is modest and appropriate for worship rather than worrying about whether you look “Orthodox enough.”

What about head coverings?

The practice of Christian women covering their heads during prayer has ancient roots and is mentioned by St. Paul in 1 Corinthians 11. The practice continues among many Orthodox Christian women today, although customs differ between Orthodox parishes and cultures.

At our parish, you may see some women wearing a scarf, veil, or other head covering during worship, while many women do not. Either is perfectly comfortable here. If you normally veil for prayer, please feel completely comfortable doing so. If you do not, you should feel equally comfortable attending without a head covering.

Visitors should never feel that they need to purchase or wear a veil before attending.

Most importantly: please come. We would much rather have you worship with us than stay home because you were worried about what to wear.

What Should I Bring?

Just yourself.

You don't need to bring anything special to attend an Orthodox service. Just come.

If you're accustomed to attending a Protestant church, you may be used to bringing your Bible with you each Sunday. You're certainly welcome to bring one, but don't be surprised if you don't see many people carrying Bibles into the service. Scripture is woven throughout Orthodox worship, and the appointed Epistle and Gospel readings for the day are proclaimed during the Divine Liturgy.

If you'd like, you can read the day's Scripture readings before you arrive.

There is a Divine Liturgy book waiting in the pew to help you follow the service. You're welcome to use it as much or as little as you'd like.

No Bible to pack. No supplies to bring. No homework to finish. Just come and worship with us.

What About Bringing Children?

Bring them. Children belong in the life of the Church.

Children of every age are welcome in our services. Orthodox worship is something we experience together as a family, and we believe children learn to worship by being present for worship — seeing the icons, hearing the hymns, smelling the incense, making the sign of the Cross, and growing into the rhythm of the Church alongside the rest of us.

Jesus welcomed little children and taught us that we, too, must become like them. Their presence among us is not an interruption to worship; they are part of our parish family and part of our worship.

Babies cry. Toddlers wiggle. Children sometimes whisper, drop things, or need to move around. Please don't worry if your children make noise. No one expects them to be perfectly quiet for the entire service.

If your child needs a break, you are always welcome to step out and return when you're ready — but please never feel that you need to leave simply because your child is being a child.

If attending the entire Sunday morning service would be difficult for your family, many families with young children choose to arrive closer to the beginning of Divine Liturgy rather than attending all of Orthros beforehand.

Visiting during the school year?

After receiving Communion, you'll see many of our children head to Sunday School. Classes continue through the remainder of the service until our Agape Meal begins. If you're visiting with children, a greeter can help explain where the classes meet and what options are available for your family.

Your children are welcome here, too.

What Happens After Communion?

They aren't leaving for the day — they're going to Sunday School.

During the school year, you may notice many of our children leaving the church together after Holy Communion. Don't worry—they aren't leaving for the day! They're heading to their Sunday School classes.

Sunday School begins after Communion and continues until the Divine Liturgy has concluded and it is time for our Agape Meal (coffee hour). The children then rejoin their families for food and fellowship in the parish hall.

If you're visiting with children and would like to know whether they may join a class, or simply want to learn more about our Sunday School program, please ask one of our greeters when you arrive. They'll be happy to help you.

When You Arrive

Enter in peace. We'll help with the rest.

When you first enter the church, you'll find yourself in the narthex, the entrance area just before the main worship space. You'll see icons, candles, and likely a few friendly faces. Someone will be there to welcome you and help you find your way.

You're welcome to light a candle, venerate the icons, or simply make your way into the church and find a place to sit. If these practices are unfamiliar to you, there is no expectation that you participate. Take your time, observe, and do what feels comfortable.

Occasionally, you may arrive while Scripture or another reading is being proclaimed. If so, we ask that you wait quietly in the narthex until the reading has finished before entering the nave, so that those listening are not disturbed. Don't worry about knowing when to enter—one of our greeters will be happy to let you know.

Once inside, sit wherever you would like; you'll find a Divine Liturgy book in the pew. If you're unsure about something, feel free to ask the person beside you. Orthodox services include periods of standing, sitting, bowing, and making the sign of the Cross, but visitors should not feel that they need to keep pace with everyone around them. Sit when you need to. Stand when you're comfortable. Watch, listen, and pray.

Most of all, enter in peace.

The Church has often been described by the Fathers as a hospital for the soul. None of us enters because we have everything figured out. We come seeking healing, mercy, and life in Christ.

You are welcome to come and seek Him with us.

Welcome to the Nativity

We're so glad you came.

If today was your first Orthodox Divine Liturgy, you probably saw and heard things that were unfamiliar. That's okay.

Orthodoxy is not something you are expected to understand in a single visit. Below, you can learn more about what you experienced, ask us a question, tell us how we did, or find ways to stay connected.

You are always welcome to simply come, listen, pray, and let the life of the Church unfold before you.

What you saw around you

Candles · Incense · Kissing icons · The icon screen

Why do Orthodox Christians light candles?

Candles are offered with our prayers and remind us of Christ, the Light of the world.

A candle is a small offering made with a prayer — for someone you love, for someone who has died, or for yourself. The flame is also an image: Christ is the Light of the world, and a lit candle is one of the Church's oldest ways of saying so.

You are welcome to light one when you come. You are equally welcome not to.

Why is incense used?

Incense has accompanied the worship of God since biblical times and is an image of our prayers rising before Him.

Incense was offered in the Temple in Jerusalem, and the Church has never stopped offering it. “Let my prayer arise in Thy sight as incense” is sung in Orthodox services to this day.

The censer is carried towards the icons and towards the people, because both are images of God.

Why do people kiss icons?

Orthodox Christians venerate icons; we do not worship them. Worship belongs to God alone.

Venerating an icon is something like kissing a photograph of someone you love. The honour passes to the person, not to the wood and paint.

An icon is a window rather than a decoration: the one shown in it is the one who is greeted.

What is the iconostasis?

The screen of icons standing between the nave, where you were, and the altar.

It is not there to hide the altar so much as to show you who is gathered at it. To the right of the central doors are always Christ and St. John the Baptist; to the left, always the Theotokos and the saint or the feast the church is named for — here, her Nativity.

Those central doors are called the Royal Door. The Gospel book and the gifts come out to the people through them, and return through them.

What people around you were doing

The sign of the Cross · Standing · Bowing · Kissing the priest's hand · Touching his robe

Why do people make the sign of the Cross?

The sign of the Cross is a physical confession of our faith in the Holy Trinity and in Christ's saving Cross.

The thumb and first two fingers are joined to confess the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit — three, and one. The remaining two fingers are folded to the palm, confessing that Christ is both God and man.

It is prayer made with the body, not a gesture that has to be got right. No visitor is expected to join in.

Worship, on our parish site →

Why is everyone standing?

Standing is the ancient posture of Christian prayer — the posture of the Resurrection.

Standing is the preferred posture of prayer in the Orthodox Church, and on a Sunday it carries a particular meaning: the canons of the Church set kneeling aside on the day of the Resurrection.

You will still see people kneel at the consecration of the gifts. That is its own act of reverence, not a mistake — worship here moves between standing, sitting, bowing and kneeling.

Please do not feel you must keep pace with everyone around you. Sit when you need to, stand when you are comfortable, and pray.

Why do people bow?

A bow is a small act of reverence, made when something holy is named or carried past.

You will see people bow their head at the naming of the Holy Trinity, when the priest gives a blessing, when the Gospel book or the gifts are carried through the church, and before venerating an icon.

Like the sign of the Cross, it is the body praying along with the words.

Why do people kiss the priest's hand?

The hand that is kissed is the hand that blesses and that holds the chalice. The honour is to the priesthood, not to the man.

When Orthodox Christians ask for a blessing they hold out their palms, right over left, and say “Father, bless.” As he blesses, the priest's fingers form the letters ΙΣ ΧΣ — Jesus Christ — so the blessing he gives is Christ's own, and that is what the kiss is for.

Visitors are not expected to. Fr. John is just as glad to be greeted with a handshake.

Why did people reach out and touch the priest's robe?

In imitation of the woman in the Gospel who was healed by touching the hem of Christ's garment.

If you saw this, it was during the Great Entrance, as the priest carried the gifts through the church. People reach out to touch his vestment as he passes; some take hold of it and kiss it.

It remembers the woman who had been ill for twelve years and said to herself, “If I only touch his garment, I shall be made well” — and was. What is reached for is not the cloth, and not the man. It is Christ passing by.

If you would like to join in, reach — but do not tug, do not step into the procession, and do not move anyone aside. You are equally welcome to stand still and pray as he goes past.

What you heard

“Kyrie eleison” · Greek and English

What does “Kyrie eleison” mean?

It means “Lord, have mercy,” one of the most ancient and frequent prayers of the Church.

You will have heard it many times over: Κύριε, ἐλέησον. It is among the shortest prayers the Church prays and by far the most repeated — ten, twenty, a hundred times in a single service.

The mercy being asked for is not only pardon. The Greek word, eleos, shares a root with the old Greek word for olive oil — what you put on a wound. It is what someone asks of God when they are hurt, as much as when they are sorry.

Why are parts of the service in Greek?

Worship here is approximately 50% Greek and 50% English. You do not need to know Greek to pray with us.

Orthodoxy belongs to no one nation. The Church has always prayed in the language of the people who are praying, and Orthodox worship is offered today in a great many languages. Here you heard two.

Keeping the Greek keeps something the parish was given and has carried since it began. The parts the congregation prays together — the Creed, the Lord's Prayer — are offered in more than one language, so that everyone gathered can pray them. A Greek-English Divine Liturgy book is available so you can follow either side.

The shape of the service

Orthros · The Divine Liturgy · The two processions · Holy Communion · Antidoron

What is Orthros?

Orthros, also called Matins, is the Church's morning service of prayer. It comes first, at 9:00 a.m.

Orthros is the service of prayer and preparation that precedes the Divine Liturgy: psalms, hymns, Scripture and prayers reflecting the feast or the saint commemorated that day.

If you arrived while it was already under way, you arrived at the right time — many people do. Visitors are welcome at Orthros, and equally welcome to come closer to the start of the Liturgy.

Sunday morning, step by step →

What is the Divine Liturgy?

The central worship service of the Orthodox Church, and the one you came to. It begins at 10:00 a.m.

The Liturgy celebrated on most Sundays is the one attributed to St. John Chrysostom, Archbishop of Constantinople in the fifth century. Its core goes back to Christ and the Apostles; the prayers, hymns and gestures around that core were added over centuries, and the shape you heard was settled by about the ninth. Scripture is read and proclaimed, the Creed is confessed, and the bread and wine are offered and become the Holy Eucharist.

It is not a lecture with music around it. It is the whole parish doing one thing together, and you were part of it by being there.

What was the procession with the Gospel?

The Little Entrance — the Gospel book carried out to the people before it is read to them.
The Little Entrance

Near the beginning of the Liturgy the Gospel book is carried out from the altar, through the church, and back in through the central doors of the icon screen.

The book is not a prop. The Gospel is Christ's own word, so the procession is Christ coming out among His people before He is proclaimed to them.

What was the procession with the bread and wine?

The Great Entrance — the parish's offering carried to the altar, to be offered.
The Great Entrance

Later in the service the bread and wine that have been prepared are carried from the table of preparation, through the church, to the altar. The congregation stands, and many bow as they pass.

They are not yet the Body and Blood of Christ at that moment. They are what the parish has brought, on its way to be offered.

Why couldn't I receive Holy Communion?

Holy Communion is the centre of Orthodox Christian worship and is received by Orthodox Christians who are prepared to receive according to the practice and pastoral guidance of the Church. Our visitors are deeply welcome even when they are not able to approach the chalice.

Holy Communion is received by Orthodox Christians who are properly prepared to receive the Eucharist. If you are not Orthodox, you were asked to remain in your place while Communion was given.

This is not a judgement about you, and it is not a way of keeping you at arm's length. Orthodox Christians understand the Eucharist as the expression of a unity of faith already shared — so receiving it together would say something the Church cannot yet say. You were entirely welcome to worship and to pray with us, and there was blessed bread for you a few minutes later.

If this is something you would like to talk about rather than read about, Fr. John would be glad to. The parish office will arrange it — see Contact.

What was the bread at the end?

This is called antidoron, or blessed bread. It is not Holy Communion, and visitors are welcome to receive it.

Antidoron is bread that has been blessed and is given to everyone present, including visitors who are not Orthodox. Fr. John normally hands it out as the Liturgy concludes and people leave the church for the hall — so you will have passed him on your way out.

If you were not sure whether it was meant for you: it was.

Who we pray with

The Theotokos · The saints

Who is the Theotokos?

“Theotokos” means “God-bearer” or “Mother of God” and is the Church's ancient title for the Virgin Mary.

The title is first of all a statement about Christ: the one she bore is God. The Church confessed it at the Council of Ephesus in 431 for exactly that reason.

This parish is named for her Nativity — her birth — which the Church celebrates on 8 September.

Why do Orthodox Christians ask saints to pray for them?

For the same reason you might ask a friend to pray for you. The saints are alive in Christ, and death does not put them out of reach.

Asking a saint to pray is not worship, and it does not stand in for praying to God yourself. It is the Church on earth and the Church in heaven praying about the same thing at the same time.

Questions are welcome.

Please stay awhile

Our life together does not end when the Divine Liturgy concludes.

Visitors are warmly invited to join us in the parish hall for coffee, food and fellowship. It is potluck-style, you need bring nothing, nobody is counted at the door, and you may leave whenever you need to. It is the easiest place to ask the ordinary questions you did not want to ask during the service.

If you have children, they are welcome too. Children's activity boxes are available outside the parish bookstore, and families are part of the life of our parish.

The bookstore is open every Sunday after the Divine Liturgy. It carries Orthodox books and pamphlets, prayer books, icons, prayer ropes, incense, crosses, candles and other devotional items for the home — including books written for people just beginning to learn about the Orthodox faith.

None of this is expected of you. If you needed to leave straight after the Liturgy, that is completely fine, and we hope to see you again.

You don't have to understand everything today.

Orthodox Christianity is not something to rush through. Come back. Ask questions. Listen. Pray. Experience the feasts and fasts, the prayers, the saints, and the rhythm of the Church year.

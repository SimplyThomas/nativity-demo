Parish life

Get Involved

Parish life is something we build together. We worship together, share meals, care for one another, prepare the church for its feasts, make offerings, learn traditions, and pass those traditions on. Whether you have been part of The Nativity for decades or walked through our doors for the first time last Sunday, there is a place for you.

You don’t have to already know how.

If something here interests you, come and learn it. Someone can show you.

Every Sunday

Sunday coffee hour

Stay awhile.

Every Sunday after the Divine Liturgy, everyone is invited to the parish hall for coffee, food and fellowship. Visitors are included in that — you need bring nothing at all, and you may leave whenever you need to.

Coffee hour is where we welcome people who have come for the first time, catch up with friends, meet the ones we have not met yet, and spend an unhurried hour together as a parish family.

Parish households help make it possible by taking turns to sponsor the Sunday coffee hour. Once a month the whole parish would bring it together instead — see the Agape Meal below.

What does a coffee hour host provide?

The parish has not published what a host is asked to bring, what the kitchen already provides, or what it usually costs, so nothing is stated here. Those are the details this section is waiting for, and they are the ones that decide whether somebody who has never done it before feels able to offer.

Until they are confirmed, the parish office can tell you what is involved and put you in touch with someone who has hosted before.

Once a month

The Agape Meal

Come to the table.

View upcoming parish events →
Photograph to come A long table in the parish hall — food being passed, several generations sitting together

Agape is the Greek word for self-giving love.

From the earliest days of the Church, Christians gathered not only for prayer and the Eucharist but around a common table, at meals known as agape — love feasts. The Eucharist and the community meal became distinct very early, by the end of the first century or the beginning of the second. What has continued ever since is the plainer thing underneath: that sitting down to eat together is how a household knows itself to be one.

So this is not an attempt to put back together something the Church deliberately separated, and it asks nothing of anybody at the Liturgy. It is a parish supper that remembers where the habit came from.

Once a month, we would like to make our Sunday fellowship especially intentional by gathering for a full parish Agape Meal after the Divine Liturgy. Rather than one household carrying the whole of coffee hour, everyone who is able brings something to share.

Bring a favourite dish.

Bring your family.

Sit with someone you do not know yet.

Welcome the person who came for the first time.

Stay a little longer.

There is room at the table for you.

We have just come from the celebration of the Divine Liturgy, and like our spiritual ancestors in the Church of the First Century, we now come to an Agape Meal.

His Eminence Archbishop Elpidophoros of AmericaFollowing the Divine Liturgy at Holy Trinity Greek Orthodox Cathedral, New Orleans · 16 March 2025

Why an Agape Meal?

Sunday fellowship can easily become something we pass through on the way to the car. Once a month, we would like to slow it down.

A shared meal makes room for the things a Sunday morning otherwise leaves out: eating together rather than standing with a cup, welcoming a visitor properly, letting the children meet the rest of the parish, and friendships across generations that the Liturgy itself gives no occasion to form.

The aim is not a larger coffee hour. The aim is fellowship.

Proposed — not yet in place

The littlest seats at the table

Children are part of the parish family too. A Kids' Corner during fellowship — books, colouring, quiet play, and now and then something to make that belongs to the saint or the feast of the day — would make it easier for families to stay, eat, and be together.

Parents would stay responsible for their own children. The corner is not childcare, and is not meant to take anyone's children off their hands; it is meant to make an hour at the table possible with them.

The Kids' Corner does not exist yet. It is described here as a proposal, alongside the people it would need — see Serve the parish below.

Prepare & beautify the church

There are many hands behind the beauty of our worship.

Flowers around an icon, greenery and candles, and the work that goes into preparing the church for the Nativity, for Holy Week, for Pascha and for the feasts through the year are acts of love and service offered by members of the parish.

You do not need to wait until somebody asks you personally. If you would like to help, we would like to show you how.

Some of this work happens inside the church and around things set apart for worship, so it is arranged with Father rather than signed up for. That is not a way of keeping anyone out — it is how the parish makes sure somebody is beside you the first time.

Prosphora

Bake the bread

Learning the tradition of prosphora.

The bread offered for the Divine Liturgy does not simply appear on Sunday morning. Members of our parish prepare and offer prosphora for the celebration of the Eucharist, and they were all taught by somebody.

If you would like to learn, Presbytera offers prosphora-baking classes teaching our parish's own practice and requirements. After learning to prepare prosphora the way this parish does it, parishioners can ask about joining the baking rotation.

It is not a difficult thing to learn. It is a particular thing to learn, which is not the same, and it is why the class comes first.

Learn the traditions

Traditions survive when they are shared.

Living the Traditions →

Many parts of Orthodox life are learned not from a book but from another person. Someone once taught us to bake prosphora. Someone showed us how koliva is prepared. Someone taught us to fold a palm cross. Someone showed us how the church is made ready for a feast.

We would like those traditions to go on being handed from one generation to the next — which means somebody has to be taught, and somebody has to do the teaching.

You do not need to already know how.

Come and learn.

Ideas, not a timetable

What we might learn together

Prosphora baking, koliva, palm crosses, red Pascha eggs, Vasilopita, Lazarakia, preparing for Holy Week and Pascha, a home prayer corner, the responses of the Divine Liturgy, the Greek heard every Sunday, and more besides.

Prosphora baking is the one the parish already offers. Everything else on that list is a possibility being put to the parish, not a class with a date. Nobody has been scheduled and nobody has been signed up to teach.

Do you know a tradition you could teach?

Some of the most valuable knowledge in our parish may never have been written down.

If you know how to prepare a traditional food, make something used in parish life, decorate for a feast, fold palms, arrange flowers, or keep another Orthodox tradition that could be passed on, we would like to hear from you. Offering does not commit you to running a class, and you do not have to be an expert — twenty minutes at a table showing four people how you do it is a real contribution.

What would you like to learn?

Is there an Orthodox tradition you wish someone had taught you — or something you learned from your parents or grandparents that you would like to see passed on?

Suggesting one takes a moment, and it is how the parish finds out which traditions people actually want. You can say whether you would come, whether you would help organise it, and leave your name out entirely if you would rather.

There is a place for you here.

You may know how to bake the bread. You may know how to arrange the flowers. You may have a recipe someone taught you forty years ago. You may be the person who sets up the tables. You may bring dinner to a family with a new baby.

Or you may be brand new, and know none of these things yet.

Come and learn. Then, one day, teach someone else.
